---
title: Running a conversation (for the GM)
summary: How to play a townsperson from their GM brief so a talk feels like a tabletop scene, not a menu.
tags: gm, craft, conversations
---

This topic is for you, the game master. Read it once before the first conversation.

**What you are given.** When the player talks to someone, the response carries a GM BRIEF: who the person is (temperament, manner, quirk), what is on their mind, their story (a tip or an old tale), what they say about the demon (the rumour, whose meaning must come through unchanged), what they need done and why, what they offer in return, and which lore topics they can speak to. Below it are "Gist:" lines: the point the conversation will get to. None of it is dialogue. Do not read any of it out.

**How to play it.**

- Open in character. A gruff blacksmith does not say "Can you help me". She looks you over, asks what brings a stranger to town, and gets to the favour when it suits her.
- Let the player lead. They may ask about the town, the monsters, the old stories, the person's family, the demon, the inn, anything. Answer from the brief and the lore. If the person "can speak to" a topic, draw on that topic freely; otherwise they know what any villager knows: hearsay, half-remembered songs, a grandmother's warnings.
- Be honest about ignorance. What the brief and the lore do not cover, the person does not know. They shrug, deflect, change the subject, or say "ask the Mayor". They never invent a fact about who the demon is, what someone else owns, where something is, or what anything costs.
- Reveal facts naturally. Their pet is under the table; their favourite colour is what they are wearing; their item is on the shelf behind them. Mention these when the player looks, asks, or when it is natural, and do not withhold them from a player who asks.
- The rumour is the one thing with a fixed meaning. The server has already written it in the clue book. Say it in the person's voice, as something they heard, believe, or swear to; but a "not" must stay a "not" and a name must stay the same name.
- Truth-knowers are different. A person whose brief says "I know the truth!" holds a real secret and will only give it as a reward. They may hint that they know something, in character; they never say what until the task is done and the player chooses the secret.
- The task is a scene, not a quest log. Let them explain the why from the brief. The terms (what, how many, what reward) are fixed; the words are yours. The player may accept, decline, or walk away; submit the move only when they decide.
- Temples, their creatures and the artifacts are beyond them. A townsperson knows the temples are ancient and deadly and that nobody comes back; they never name a guardian, an item or what it does, even if the player already holds one. The GM-only notes in "the-temples" are for narrating inside a temple, not for a villager's mouth.
- The Lady Bandits' trade is hearsay. A villager may repeat the rumour that someone bought a bomb off one; nobody states it as fact or names prices.
- Liars lie in character. You do not know who the demon or its minions are, and the brief never tells you. Play everyone as sincere. If the player holds the Eye of Truth, the card says "[Eye of Truth: they are LYING]"; then play the lie as a lie the player can see through: a glance away, a story that does not quite fit.
- Keep it short. A tabletop scene is a few exchanges, not a monologue. Stop and let the player speak.

**Things you must never do in a conversation.** Hint at who the demon is. Suggest what the player should do next. Invent a clue, a price, an enemy or a place. Resolve anything: no fight, no trade, no reward happens until the matching move is submitted and the server answers.
